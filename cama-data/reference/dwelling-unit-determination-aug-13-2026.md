# Dwelling Unit Determination

[Act 170 of 2026, An act relating to next steps in transforming Vermont’s education system](https://legislature.vermont.gov/Documents/2026/Docs/ACTS/ACT170/ACT170%As%Enacted.pdf), made changes to the information municipalities are required to submit to the Education Grand List. Starting in 2027, municipalities must complete a separate column on the Education Grand List that shows the number of dwelling units on each parcel. This field will originate from the town’s Computer Assisted Mass Appraisal (CAMA) system, which will then be transmitted into the NEMRC Grand List system as part of the existing CAMA upload.

## Dwelling Unit Definition

“Dwelling unit” is defined in the new law to mean the following:

* A building or part of a building, including a single-family home, a unit within a multifamily building, an apartment, a condominium, or other similar property or structure.
* The unit must have the following characteristics to be considered a dwelling:
  * It contains a separate means of ingress and egress. In other words, its own separate entrance. A room within a home that cannot be accessed from the outside does not count as a dwelling unit.
  * It is designed, intended, or suitable for habitation by one or more persons in a household. This means the unit provides living facilities for sleeping, cooking, and sanitary needs.
  * It is fit for year-round habitation.

This definition is exclusively for determining the number of dwelling units on a parcel. It does not directly affect property valuation or taxation. A "parcel" means all contiguous land under the same ownership, together with all improvements on that land.

Reminder: Just because an owner declares a parcel as their homestead does not necessarily mean there is a dwelling unit on the property for purposes of the Education Grand List. Any parcel can be declared as a homestead, whether or not it fits the definition of "dwelling unit" above. What matters for identifying a dwelling unit is how the unit could be used based on its characteristics, not how it is being used. For more information, see [Dwelling Unit Determination FAQs](https://tax.vermont.gov/property/classification/dwelling-unit-determination#faqs).

### Definition of Fit for Year-Round Habitation

A dwelling unit is fit for year-round habitation, or is livable, if it _could_ be inhabited year-round, not if it _is_ inhabited year-round. An individual owner’s actual use or preference is irrelevant to this determination.

Evidence that a unit is fit for year-round habitation includes a sufficient heating system, insulation or weatherization, and indoor plumbing and running water that is useable year-round. A property’s permitted or zoned use is not a decisive factor to show year-round habitability for purposes of identifying dwelling units on the Education Grand List. A property must also be reasonably accessible year-round by people and motor vehicles to be considered a dwelling unit.

If a unit is not fit for year-round habitation, which is the case for many camps and other similar properties in Vermont, then it is not a dwelling unit and should not be counted as such on the Education Grand List. For more information, see [Dwelling Unit Determination FAQs](https://tax.vermont.gov/property/classification/dwelling-unit-determination#faqs).

#### Heating, Insulation, and Weatherization

For a unit to be considered habitable year-round, it must be sufficiently equipped to be heated through Vermont winters. Any type of year-round heating system qualifies; for example, wood stoves, or propane, oil, and wood pellet furnaces or boilers. A unit equipped with only heat pumps will be unlikely to qualify as fit for year-round habitation because most heat pumps are not designed to withstand the extended subfreezing temperatures typical of Vermont winters without backup heating.

If a unit has no insulation, is not enclosed, or is open to the elements, then it is not fit for year-round habitation and is not considered a dwelling unit.

#### Plumbing and Running Water

A unit must have indoor running water, plumbing, and septic, or another wastewater system that can operate year-round and is sufficient for the size of the unit to be considered a dwelling unit.

The plumbing must provide water inside the dwelling and must be usable year-round. This means main lines must be below the frost line or otherwise insulated to operate year-round. A septic or wastewater system must be available inside the dwelling unit and must be capable of year-round operation. It does not matter what the type of system is, just that it is capable of handling the sanitary needs of a unit of that size.

#### Access

A unit must be reasonably accessible year-round by people and motor vehicles to be considered a dwelling unit. The type of road (public or private) also does not matter, nor does it matter if the municipality maintains the road in the winter. For example, a unit that is only accessible via a Class 4 road that is not maintained or plowed by the town in the winter can still be considered a dwelling unit if the unit could be reasonably plowed, maintained, and accessed by the owner. If a road is so steep that it cannot be plowed in the winter, or is impassable in mud season, then it is not accessible year-round, and any unit located on that road is not a dwelling unit.

#### Permitting or Zoning

Local or State permitting or zoning allowing or limiting a property’s use does not impact the determination of whether a unit is a dwelling unit for purposes of the Education Grand List. For example, a local ordinance that prohibits year-round habitation of units that do not have enough off-street parking is immaterial to whether that unit is a dwelling unit. Similarly, if a unit is fit for year-round habitation but is located in an area zoned only for seasonal camps (a non-conforming use) such as around a lake, then it can still be a dwelling unit.

#### Construction or Rehabilitation

A building is not fit for year-round habitation if it is under construction and still unfinished or is undergoing rehabilitation to the extent that it becomes uninhabitable for humans as of April 1 of the grand list year. An uninhabitable building is not a dwelling unit for property tax purposes.

## Dwelling Unit Determination FAQs

Updated August 13, 2026

**Q1. Why do towns need to start identifying dwelling units?**

Act 170 of 2026, an act relating to next steps in transforming Vermont’s education system, defined dwelling units and requires a dwelling unit count to be on the Grand List starting in 2027 as a first step towards taxing properties based on how they are used. The new law also created a new property tax classification called “nonhomestead residential” effective July 1, 2029 (if several contingencies are met). This new classification would apply to second homes and short-term rentals. See [Nonhomestead Residential Classification Timeline](https://tax.vermont.gov/property/classification) for more information.

Identifying dwelling units is a necessary building block for the future classification structure.

**Q2. What is a "dwelling unit" for the purposes of property tax classification?**

Act 170 created a new definition of “dwelling unit” in law. The new law defines a dwelling unit as:

> (2) “Dwelling unit” means a building or part of a building, including a single-family home, a unit within a multifamily building, an apartment, a condominium, or other similar property or structure containing a separate means of ingress and egress that:
>
>      (A) is designed or intended to be used for occupancy by one or more persons in a household, including providing living facilities for sleeping, cooking, and sanitary needs; and
>
>      (B) is fit for year-round habitation as determined by the Commissioner.

**Q3. Is a “dwelling unit” for the purposes of property tax classification different from “dwelling” for purposes of other State laws like homestead or Current Use?**

The definition of “dwelling unit” in Act 170 is only used for determining the number of dwelling units on a parcel. It does not directly affect property valuation or taxation.

It does not have the same meaning as “dwelling” for purposes of determining a homestead. Any parcel can be declared as a homestead, whether or not it fits the definition of "dwelling unit" above, including buildings that are not fit for year-round habitation like camps.

It also does not have the same meaning as “dwelling” in the Current Use program, which applies to different types of buildings and uses.

The definition of “dwelling” in other legal contexts come from different laws with different purposes, requirements, and uses. What matters for property tax classification is how the dwelling unit could be used based on its characteristics; not how it is actually being used.

**Q4. What does “fit for year-round habitation” mean?**

A dwelling unit is fit for year-round habitation, or is livable, if it could be inhabited year-round. It does not matter if the dwelling unit is inhabited year-round. An individual owner’s actual use or preference is irrelevant to this determination.

Evidence that a unit is fit for year-round habitation includes:

* a sufficient heating system,
* insulation or weatherization,  
* indoor plumbing and running water that is useable year-round, and
* reasonable year-round access to the dwelling unit for people and motor vehicles.

A property’s permitted or zoned use is not a decisive factor to show year-round habitability for purposes of identifying dwelling units on the Education Grand List.

If a unit is not fit for year-round habitation, which is the case for many camps and other similar properties in Vermont, then it is not a dwelling unit and should not be counted as such on the Education Grand List.

**Q5. My road is private and not plowed by the town. Is that “reasonable year-round access” for motor vehicles?**

Reasonable access to a dwelling unit is a determining factor for year-round habitability. The type of road (public or private) does not matter, nor does it matter if the municipality maintains the road in the winter. It does not matter what the property owner’s actual maintenance practices are. It only matters whether the road _could_ reasonably be accessed with maintenance.

For example, a unit that is only accessible via a Class 4 road that is not maintained or plowed by the town in the winter can still be considered a dwelling unit if the unit could be reasonably plowed, maintained, and accessed by the owner.

Situations without “reasonable year-round access” include if a road is so steep that it cannot be plowed in the winter, or if a road is impassable in mud season. Any unit located on a road like that is not a dwelling unit.  

**Q6. Does a building have to have electricity to be considered fit for year-round habitation?**

Electricity is not required for a dwelling unit to be considered fit for year-round habitation as long as the dwelling unit meets all of the other requirements of the definition, such as a sufficient heating system. A lister or assessor should use their professional judgment to determine what is habitable.

**Q7. What constitutes providing living facilities for sleeping, cooking, and sanitary needs? Is a hot plate and a fridge enough to provide cooking facilities?**

Depending on the presence or absence of other living facilities, a hot plate and a refrigerator could provide sufficient cooking facilities for a dwelling unit.

Only the parts of a building that are designed to be occupied by people can be considered dwelling units under the new law. Each unit must provide facilities for people to live in and take care of their basic physical needs. The law requires space for:

* sleeping, which means a bedroom or other sleeping area;
* space for cooking, which means a kitchen or other food preparation space; and
* space for sanitary needs, which means a bathroom or another zone with a toilet and bathing accommodations.

In most cases, the presence or absence of these facilities will be obvious. In unclear situations, it will be up to the lister or assessor to use their professional judgment to determine if there is a dwelling unit based on the overall facts and circumstances. If the cooking facilities in a dwelling unit consist of a hot plate and a fridge, but all other living facilities are present, then the unit is likely a dwelling unit. If no other facilities are present, however, then the overall facts and circumstances will point to the unit not being a dwelling unit.

**Q8. I have an apartment building with six apartments. Is each its own dwelling unit?**

It is likely that an apartment building with six apartments contains six dwelling units, but that determination can only be made for sure by evaluating each apartment separately using the dwelling unit definition. The law expressly names units within multifamily buildings, apartments, and condominiums as buildings or parts of buildings that can be dwelling units.

If each unit meets the definition in the law, then it will be considered a dwelling unit that has to be counted on the Grand List.

**Q9. Are mobile and manufactured homes dwelling units?**

Yes, mobile and manufactured homes are considered dwelling units as long as they are fit for year-round habitation. They are a type of building that has a separate entrance and is designed to be occupied by people, meaning they have space for sleeping, cooking, and sanitary waste disposal.

**Q10. Are outbuildings dwelling units?**

Outbuildings or any structure or improvement are dwelling units if they meet the definition of a dwelling unit. To be a dwelling unit, an outbuilding must have a separate entrance, be designed to be occupied by people (meaning it has space for sleeping, cooking, and sanitary waste disposal), and be fit for year-round habitation. To determine if a unit is a dwelling unit, see [Dwelling Unit Determination](https://tax.vermont.gov/sites/tax/files/documents/FL-1363.pdf).
